## Key Takeaways

- **Fundamental analysis** decides **what** deserves attention.
- **Seasonal analysis** decides **when** the odds have historically been favorable.
- **Technical analysis** helps **execute**: timing the entry and managing risk.
- Each lens has a classic failure mode. Fundamentals without timing can become dead money. Technicals without context can become random signal hunting. Seasonality without discipline can become overconfidence.

## The Three Lenses at a Glance

| Lens | The question it answers | What it studies | Best use | Classic failure |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fundamental | What is it worth? | Earnings, revenue, margins, balance sheet | Choosing quality long-term holdings | Right company, years of waiting |
| Technical | How should I execute? | Price, volume, trend, momentum | Timing entries and exits over days to weeks | Indicators that confirm after the move is done |
| Seasonal | When are the odds favorable? | The same calendar window across many years | Choosing when and which direction to look | Trusting the calendar without a stop |

## Fundamental Analysis: The Business Lens

Fundamental analysis looks at the business itself. Is revenue growing? Are margins expanding? Is the balance sheet strong? Is the stock cheap or expensive relative to its earnings?

It is the right tool for deciding which companies are worth owning for years. Its weakness is timing. Value is slow to change and prices can ignore it for long stretches, so a correct fundamental thesis can still sit flat or fall for a year or more.

## Technical Analysis: The Price Lens

Technical analysis studies the chart: trend, support and resistance, moving averages, momentum and volume. Its premise is that price already reflects information and that widely watched signals can become self-fulfilling.

It is the right tool for timing an entry and placing a stop. Its weakness is that it is reactive. By the time several indicators line up, much of the move may already be over, and in a news-driven shock many signals fail together.

## Seasonal Analysis: The Calendar Lens

Seasonal analysis asks a narrower question: during this exact calendar window, across many years, what did this market tend to do, how often and how strongly? It is often the most underused of the three lenses, mainly because testing it properly takes a lot of data and computation.

It is the right tool for deciding **when** to look and in **which direction**. Its weakness is that a calendar has no stop-loss. A window that worked in 9 of 10 years still failed once, and history does not protect you from this year's surprise. See [What Is Seasonal Trading?](/learn/what-is-seasonal-trading.html)

## How to Combine Them

A simple layered process uses each lens for the job it does best:

1. **Fundamentals pick the universe.** Focus on companies or ETFs you understand and would be comfortable owning.
2. **Seasonality picks the window and the direction.** Look for windows with a strong, consistent record in the right [election-cycle lens](/learn/presidential-election-cycle-regimes.html).
3. **Quality metrics filter out weak patterns.** Favor windows with a solid [Sharpe Ratio](/learn/sharpe-ratio-explained.html) and [TradeWave Ratio](/learn/tradewave-ratio-twr-and-twa.html), not just a high win rate.
4. **Technicals confirm the entry.** Wait for price to agree with the calendar, for example holding above the 50-day moving average inside a long window.
5. **Risk rules finish the job.** Plan the stop, target and exit date before entering. See [Position Sizing and Stop Losses](/learn/position-sizing-and-stop-loss-seasonal-trades.html).

This structure exists to prevent two common errors: **the right pattern in the wrong environment**, and **the right window with the wrong entry**.

## When the Lenses Disagree

If the calendar says up and the chart says down hard, history and the present are in conflict. The disciplined responses are to pass, reduce size, or wait for price to confirm. Standing aside is a position too. When everything lines up, you can act with more conviction, but you still use a stop.
