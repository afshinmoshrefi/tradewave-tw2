## Key Takeaways

- A **seasonal pattern** is a recurring tendency for a market to move in one direction around the same calendar dates.
- Real patterns are driven by real cycles: holiday spending, fiscal budgets, earnings calendars, harvests, tax flows, weather-driven energy demand and election-cycle policy timing.
- In TradeWave a pattern is defined by **five things together**: symbol, start date, days held, year set and direction.
- A pattern becomes useful only when you measure how **often** it worked, how **large** the move was and how **messy** it got when it failed.
- Seasonality is a probability, not a prediction.

## A Plain-English Definition

A seasonal pattern says something like: *"From February 5 through March 17, this stock finished higher in most of the years we can measure."* It is a calendar window plus a historical track record.

Seasonal patterns are not magic and they are not astrology. They exist because a lot of economic activity runs on a calendar, and money moves when that activity happens. Some of the most common drivers:

- **Consumer and holiday spending**, which concentrates retail revenue late in the year
- **Corporate fiscal years**, budgets and earnings seasons
- **Agricultural planting and harvest cycles**
- **Inventory and supply-chain cycles**
- **Tax-driven flows** and institutional rebalancing at quarter and year end
- **Energy demand tied to weather and travel**, such as the summer driving season
- **Election-cycle policy timing**, which TradeWave treats as its own lens (see [the Presidential Election Cycle Lens](/learn/presidential-election-cycle-regimes.html))

One useful way to think about it: day-to-day price moves look like noise, but underneath there is a repeating chord progression. Seasonality is a way of hearing it.

## The Five Parts That Define a Pattern

In TradeWave, a pattern is uniquely identified by five settings. Change any one of them and you are looking at a different pattern with a different track record.

| Part | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Symbol** | The stock, ETF, index, future or currency | CAH |
| **Start date** | The calendar day the window opens | February 5 |
| **Days held** | How long the window lasts, in calendar days | 41 days |
| **Year set** | Which historical years are measured | the last 10 midterm years |
| **Direction** | Long (expect a rise) or short (expect a fall) | Long |

TradeWave windows are measured in **calendar days**, and the entry day counts as day 1. A 41-day window that starts February 5 ends on March 17.

### Why the Year Set Matters So Much

"The last 10 years" and "the last 10 midterm-election years" are two completely different datasets. The first covers a decade. The second spans about forty years, because a midterm election comes once every four years. A pattern that looks strong in one set can disappear in the other. That is not a bug; it is often the most important information on the screen. [How Many Years of Data Do You Need?](/learn/how-many-years-of-data-seasonal-backtest.html) explains how to choose.

## The Three Measurements That Make a Pattern Useful

Knowing that a window "tends to go up" is trivia. It becomes a decision tool when you can answer three questions:

1. **How often did it work?** The win rate, written as a count such as 9 of 10 years, so it stays a historical fact rather than a forecast.
2. **How large was the move?** The average gain and, more importantly, the [median gain](/learn/median-vs-average-return.html), which is not distorted by one spectacular year.
3. **How messy did it get?** The volatility, the losing years and the worst drawdown inside the window ([MAE](/learn/mfe-and-mae-explained.html)).

The Wave Viewer shows all three: the Profit Bar Chart for the year-by-year record, the Trend Chart for the typical path, and the Stats Table for the numbers.

## What a Seasonal Pattern Is Not

- **It is not a guarantee.** A window that worked in 9 of 10 years still failed once, and this year could be the exception.
- **It is not an entry signal by itself.** Seasonality sets the bias. Price action, your stop and your position size still decide the trade.
- **It is not a reason to ignore news.** A pattern describes normal behavior. A genuine shock can override it, which is why every seasonal trade still needs a planned exit.
