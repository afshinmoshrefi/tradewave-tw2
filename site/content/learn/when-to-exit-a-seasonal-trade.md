## Key Takeaways

- Plan **three exits before entering**: a profit target, a stop loss and an exit date.
- The **end of the window is a time stop**. When the seasonal reason for the trade expires, so does the trade.
- Seasonal moves tend to **peak and fade**. Holding past the peak zone often hands the gain back.
- **Panic exits** are the mirror-image mistake. Historical context shows whether a dip is normal for this window.
- Traders who plan exits execute them. Traders who do not, improvise.

## The Three Planned Exits

| Exit | What triggers it | Where the number comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Profit target | Price reaches your target | The window's typical favorable move ([MFE](/learn/mfe-and-mae-explained.html)), set conservatively |
| Stop loss | Price reaches your stop | Beyond the adverse move winning years usually endured (MAE). See [Position Sizing and Stop Losses](/learn/position-sizing-and-stop-loss-seasonal-trades.html) |
| Time stop | The window's end date arrives | The pattern definition itself |

Whichever comes first ends the trade.

## Why the Window End Matters

A seasonal trade exists because of a calendar tendency. Once the window closes, the historical reason to hold is gone, and anything after that is a different, untested bet.

The data shows how costly overstaying can be. In one TradeWave example, a 17-day window had a Sharpe Ratio of 2.18, a perfect win record and a cumulative return of about +102%. Stretching the same window to 86 days dropped the Sharpe Ratio to about zero, the win rate to 60% and the cumulative return to about -2%. The extra 69 days did not add opportunity; they gave it back.

## Finding the Peak Zone

The [Trend Chart](/learn/understanding-the-trend-chart.html) shows the typical path through the window. Most seasonal moves have three phases:

1. **Pivot zone:** where the average bends and the move begins.
2. **Run:** the bulk of the gain.
3. **Peak zone:** where the typical path flattens or rolls over.

If the peak usually arrives before the formal end date, consider taking profits into the peak zone or tightening your stop as you approach it. If the window's MFE is routinely much larger than its final return, that is a sign the move tends to fade late.

## Avoiding Panic Exits

The opposite error is selling a good trade during a normal dip. Many winning years dip before they work, and the Profit Bar Chart with MAE shown makes that visible.

TradeWave's research includes a case where a semiconductor stock's live year was deep in the red partway through a historically strong window, while history was strongly positive for the same dates. The Trend Chart context was right: the position recovered. A trader who sold on fear would have locked in the worst part of a normal year.

The rule: **exit on your plan, not on your nerves.** If price hits the stop, exit. If it has not, check whether the move is normal for this point in the window before doing anything.

## When to Exit Early on Purpose

There are good reasons to deviate from the plan:

- **News that changes the story**, such as a takeover, guidance cut or regulatory action. History did not include this year's event.
- **The trade has already reached a typical-year gain early.** Taking some or all of it is reasonable, especially near the peak zone.
- **Price action has broken down hard against the seasonal bias**, for example decisively below its 50-day moving average in a long window.

What is not a good reason: a few red days, a scary headline that does not change the business, or a gut feeling.
