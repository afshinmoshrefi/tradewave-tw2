## Key Takeaways

- **Seasonal trading** means taking positions during calendar windows where a market has historically tended to move in one direction.
- Seasonality exists because much of the economy runs on a calendar: holidays, fiscal years, earnings, harvests, taxes, weather and elections.
- The evidence is a historical base rate, such as "higher in 17 of the last 20 years," not a prediction.
- Good seasonal trades combine a strong historical record with discipline: a planned stop, a planned exit and sensible position size.
- The most common beginner mistakes are trusting small samples, chasing one-year wonders and holding past the end of the window.

## Seasonality in One Paragraph

Markets are driven by people and institutions, and a lot of what they do repeats on a schedule. Retailers make most of their money around the holidays. Companies spend budgets before fiscal year end. Refiners and airlines prepare for summer travel. Funds rebalance at quarter end, and investors sell losers for tax reasons in December. When the same behavior repeats around the same dates, it can leave a repeating footprint in prices. Seasonal trading tries to measure those footprints and use them.

## What the Evidence Looks Like

The classic example is the broad stock market. Across nearly a century of S&P 500 history, TradeWave's research finds that most long-term compounding happened between late October and early September, while a roughly 52-day stretch from about September 4 to October 25 averaged a small loss. Traders call that weak stretch the Lost Summer. It is the measured version of folklore like "Sell in May," which turns out to be much more specific than the slogan suggests. Our Insights essay on [the Halloween effect](/insights/halloween-effect.html) covers the academic history.

Individual stocks have their own calendars, often far stronger than the index. A semiconductor company may have a reliable window tied to its industry's order cycle. A retailer may have one tied to holiday reporting. These are the windows the [Opportunities Table](/learn/finding-high-probability-trading-waves.html) is built to surface.

## How Seasonal Trading Works in Practice

1. **Find candidates.** Scan for windows starting soon that worked in a large share of years. See [How to Find Stocks With Seasonal Patterns](/learn/how-to-find-stocks-with-seasonal-patterns.html).
2. **Check the quality of the record.** A high win rate is only the start. Check the median gain, the Sharpe Ratio and the size of the losing years. See [Reading the Numbers](/learn/#metrics).
3. **Plan the trade.** Decide the entry, the stop, the target and the exit date before you buy. See [Position Sizing and Stop Losses](/learn/position-sizing-and-stop-loss-seasonal-trades.html).
4. **Execute and review.** Enter, exit on schedule, record the result and compare it with history.

## Seasonal Trading vs Seasonal Investing

Seasonality serves two very different users:

- **Traders** use short windows, usually 10 to 120 days, around a specific stock, and judge them on win rate and risk-adjusted return.
- **Investors** use long windows, often five months or more, to decide when to be fully invested and when to step aside or hedge, and judge them on whether they beat simply buying and holding. See [Seasonal Investing for Long-Term Investors](/learn/seasonal-investing-for-long-term-investors.html).

## The Election Cycle Changes the Picture

Many seasonal patterns behave differently depending on where the market is in the four-year U.S. presidential cycle. Blending all years together can hide a strong pattern or create a fake one. TradeWave lets you measure the same window using only election years, post-election years, midterm years or pre-election years. ## Five Mistakes New Seasonal Traders Make

1. **Trusting tiny samples.** Five out of five sounds perfect, but five years is a thin record. See [How Many Years of Data Do You Need?](/learn/how-many-years-of-data-seasonal-backtest.html)
2. **Falling for a hero year.** One spectacular year can make an average look great. Compare it with the [median](/learn/median-vs-average-return.html).
3. **Curve-fitting.** Searching every possible date range until something looks amazing guarantees a false positive. See [Curve-Fitting vs Window Alignment](/learn/curve-fitting-vs-window-alignment.html).
4. **Treating the window as a guarantee.** Seasonality is probability, not protection. Every trade still needs a stop.
5. **Overstaying.** Seasonal moves tend to peak and fade. Holding past the end of the window often gives the gain back. See [When to Exit a Seasonal Trade](/learn/when-to-exit-a-seasonal-trade.html).
